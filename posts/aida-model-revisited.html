The AIDA Model Revisited: Modernizing the Classic Sales Funnel

AR
ARISE Editorial Team
April 1, 2025
12 min read
Conversion & CRO
🚀

In 1898, E. St. Elmo Lewis — an advertising pioneer who spent his career trying to understand why some ads worked and others didn't — published a model so elegant that it would outlive him by over a century. He described what he believed was the stages every customer moved through before making a purchase: capturing Attention, generating Interest, creating Desire, and motivating Action.

More than 125 years later, the AIDA model remains one of the most widely taught and applied frameworks in marketing. But the world Lewis wrote for — where direct mail was cutting-edge and television hadn't been invented — bears little resemblance to today's omnichannel, always-on digital landscape. The model needs modernization.

This guide revisits AIDA with fresh eyes: explaining what each stage means in today's context, exploring extensions that make it more powerful, and showing you how to apply it across your content strategy, email sequences, landing pages, and full-funnel campaigns.

The History and Endurance of AIDA

AIDA's durability is a testament to how deeply Lewis understood human decision-making. Despite the radical transformation of media, technology, and consumer behavior, people still move through the same fundamental psychological stages before making a purchase:

  1. They must become aware something exists (Attention)
  2. They must become curious enough to learn more (Interest)
  3. They must want it enough to consider buying (Desire)
  4. They must be compelled to take the next step (Action)

The stages are universal. What changes is the mechanism by which you move people through them. In 1898, the mechanism was a full-page newspaper ad. Today, it might be a TikTok video, a retargeting sequence, a LinkedIn post, or an automated email flow.

AIDA Explained: What Each Stage Means Today

A: Attention — Stopping the Scroll

Attention has never been more expensive or more scarce. The average person is exposed to 4,000–10,000 marketing messages per day. Your first challenge is simply to interrupt the noise and make someone pause.

What creates attention in 2025:

  • Pattern interrupts: Content that violates expectations. An unusual statistic. A contrarian headline. An unexpected visual. Anything that breaks the pattern of what users expect to see next.
  • Relevance signals: Content that immediately signals "this is for you." Specificity creates attention — "For SaaS founders with under 50 employees" grabs the right person better than "For all business owners."
  • Emotional hooks: Fear, curiosity, aspiration, and humor are the most powerful attention-getting emotions. Lead with them.
  • Strong visual hierarchy: In digital environments, users scan before they read. Your headline, subheadline, and hero image must do the attention work before any body copy.

Measurement: Impressions, reach, click-through rate (CTR), scroll depth (for content), video play rate.

I: Interest — Earning the Right to Continue

Once you have someone's attention, you have approximately 3–8 seconds to earn their sustained interest before they leave. Interest is created by demonstrating immediate relevance and value — showing the reader that continuing to engage is worth their time.

How to create interest:

  • The hook-to-value bridge: Your attention-getting hook makes a promise. Your interest-generating content fulfills that promise — immediately. Don't delay the value.
  • Empathy and acknowledgment: The fastest way to create interest is to accurately describe your reader's situation, problem, or aspiration. When people feel understood, they lean in.
  • Intrigue and curiosity gaps: Introduce a question or concept that creates a knowledge gap the reader wants to close. "Here's why your LTV calculation is probably wrong — and how it's costing you growth." The reader needs to know if they're making this mistake.
  • Credibility signals: Early social proof (testimonial quotes, customer logos, specific data points) immediately elevates interest by signaling that others have found value here.

Measurement: Time on page, scroll depth, pages per session, email open rates, video watch time.

D: Desire — Moving from "Nice to Have" to "I Need This"

Interest is intellectual engagement. Desire is emotional commitment. It's the shift from "this is interesting" to "I want this for myself." This is the most challenging stage to engineer — and the most important.

Desire-creation techniques:

  • Vivid outcome visualization: Paint a specific, tangible picture of the customer's life after using your product. Not "increase your revenue" but "imagine waking up on Monday to find your SDRs have 15 warm leads already queued from the weekend's automated outreach."
  • Social proof at scale: Case studies, testimonials, reviews, and user-generated content all answer the implicit question: "Has this worked for someone like me?" The more similar the proof source to the prospect, the more powerful the desire effect.
  • Feature-to-benefit translation: Features are logical. Benefits are emotional. "Automated email sequences" is a feature. "Never let a hot lead go cold again while you're in back-to-back meetings" is the desire-generating benefit.
  • Reciprocity and free value: Giving substantial value upfront (free tools, detailed guides, templates) creates a psychological reciprocity that makes desire feel authentic, not manufactured.

Measurement: Trial sign-ups, demo requests, product page engagement, wishlist additions, pricing page visits.

A: Action — Removing Every Barrier to Yes

Action is where all the upstream investment either converts into revenue or disappears into bounce rate statistics. The goal of this stage is to make saying "yes" as easy as possible, and to reduce the psychological and practical friction of saying it.

Action optimization principles:

  • Single, clear CTA: Every page should have one primary call to action. Multiple CTAs create decision paralysis. The paradox of choice is real — more options lead to fewer conversions.
  • Risk reversal: Free trials, money-back guarantees, and no-credit-card-required sign-ups all remove the financial risk of action. The lower the risk, the lower the barrier.
  • Urgency and scarcity: When genuine, limited-time offers or limited-availability signals create authentic urgency that moves fence-sitters to decision. Never fabricate scarcity — it destroys trust immediately when discovered.
  • Momentum micro-commitments: Break the desired action into smaller steps. Instead of "sign up and fill out a 10-field form," try "enter your email to get started" — then collect additional information progressively.

Measurement: Conversion rate, lead form completion rate, trial activation rate, revenue per visitor.

Modern Extensions to AIDA

Several modern frameworks have extended AIDA to address the realities of digital marketing:

  • AIDCAS: Adds Conviction (building certainty through evidence) and Satisfaction (post-purchase experience that drives retention and referral).
  • AIDAS: Adds Satisfaction at the end — acknowledging that the customer journey doesn't end at purchase. This aligns AIDA with the modern flywheel model.
  • See-Think-Do-Care: Google's framework maps awareness (See), consideration (Think), purchase (Do), and loyalty (Care) — effectively a more digital-native AIDA equivalent.

The most important modern extension is the recognition that Action is not the end of the funnel. In SaaS and subscription businesses, retention and advocacy are just as important as acquisition. Post-purchase AIDA — maintaining attention, sustaining interest, deepening desire, and prompting renewal and upgrade actions — is where the highest-LTV businesses differentiate themselves.

Applying AIDA to Your Content Marketing

Every piece of content can be mapped to an AIDA stage, allowing you to audit your content library for gaps:

  • Attention content: Short-form social posts, viral videos, thought leadership LinkedIn articles, podcast appearances. Goal: reach and awareness.
  • Interest content: Blog posts, newsletters, educational videos, podcast episodes. Goal: sustained engagement with your ideas.
  • Desire content: Case studies, detailed product comparisons, ROI calculators, customer testimonial videos. Goal: "I want what they have."
  • Action content: Landing pages, free trial flows, product demos, pricing pages, checkout sequences. Goal: conversion.

If your content library is 80% blog posts (Interest stage) and zero case studies (Desire stage), you have a funnel gap that's costing you conversions regardless of how good your blog traffic numbers look.

Applying AIDA to Email Sequences

Email nurture sequences are AIDA in action over time. A well-structured welcome sequence might look like:

  1. Email 1 (Attention): Welcome + your most compelling, unexpected insight. Subject: "The thing every [role] gets wrong about [problem]."
  2. Email 2 (Interest): Deep dive on the problem you solve. Tell the story of a customer who had this problem before finding you.
  3. Email 3 (Desire): Case study or testimonial. Specific results, specific numbers. "Sarah increased her team's outbound by 300% in 6 weeks."
  4. Email 4 (Action): Clear CTA to start a trial, book a demo, or speak with the team. Risk-reversal language. Time-sensitive offer if available.

Applying AIDA to Landing Pages

A high-converting landing page moves visitors through all four AIDA stages in a single scroll:

  • Above the fold (Attention + Interest): Compelling headline that makes a specific promise. Subheadline that addresses the target audience's top pain point. Strong hero visual or video.
  • Social proof section (Interest + Desire): Logos, testimonials, key statistics. Answers "who else is using this?"
  • Features and benefits (Desire): Translate features into outcomes. Use the "so that" test: "[Feature] so that [customer outcome]."
  • CTA section (Action): Bold, single CTA. Risk reversal. Reinforce the core value proposition one final time.

Limitations of AIDA: When to Use Something Else

AIDA's linear funnel model has two significant limitations in the modern digital context:

  1. Non-linear journeys: Modern buyers don't move cleanly through stages. They jump in and out, consume content out of sequence, and make decisions after 8–12 touchpoints across multiple channels. AIDA works as a design framework, but doesn't accurately map actual customer behavior.
  2. Missing retention: AIDA ends at Action. For subscription businesses, the most important commercial activity — reducing churn, driving expansion revenue, and generating referrals — happens entirely after AIDA ends.

For businesses where LTV is the primary growth lever, supplement AIDA with the AARRR framework, which explicitly models post-purchase retention and referral stages.

Conclusion: AIDA Is a Timeless Map, Not a Rigid Process

The AIDA model isn't outdated — it's incomplete on its own. When paired with modern digital marketing tools, multi-touch attribution thinking, and post-purchase loyalty strategies, it remains one of the most useful frameworks in any marketer's toolkit.

Use AIDA to audit your content mix, structure your copy, design your email sequences, and diagnose conversion problems. Then extend it with the frameworks that address its gaps: AARRR for retention measurement, the Flywheel model for advocacy, and Jobs-to-Be-Done for the psychological depth that AIDA's functional stages can't capture.

A 125-year-old framework, thoughtfully modernized, remains one of your most powerful assets.
